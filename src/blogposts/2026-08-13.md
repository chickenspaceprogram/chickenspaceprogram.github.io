# What's in a name?

Date: 26 Thermidor 234

Names are important. Most of us have one, and they're something we use on a
daily basis to refer to people. It's unlikely for two folks you know to
coincidentally have the same name, so they're a great way to disambiguate
between multiple different people. In a way, they encapsulate someone's
identity, their entire being down into a short phrase, by which you can
remember and identify them.

People, on a personal level, also tend to identify with and embody their
names. Plenty of folks feel or are told that they "look like a Claire" or that
"Andrew fits you," or something similar. Some even assign meaning to their
names, or google where and how their name originated out of curiosity. Names
are clearly a fundamental part of most folks' identities.

Already, a couple paragraphs into this, and it should be pretty clear that a
name is subjectively more than just a series of characters or an identifier.
We, as people and as a society, give them additional meaning and additional
weight. We identify with them, we mould them to fit us, and ourselves to fit
them.

For trans people, names are particularly important; being referred to with a
name you don't identify with and actively hate is, frankly, a traumatic
experience, once it gets done for long enough. Deadnaming (calling someone by
a name they no longer wish to be called) is no mere insult, it is the
revocation of someone's entire identity and the assertion that they deserve
to be hurt just because someone else More Important happens to not be fond of
their existence.

The power dynamic is inherent to deadnaming; the deadnamer must believe that
their thoughts and feelings about someone else's name take precedence over
that person's, and should thus be treated with more importance. It shouldn't
have to be said that this is unjust. Nobody's lesser than anyone else, nobody
gets the right to revoke another's identity just because they don't happen to
like it.

If you, as a cis person reading this, think this is an exaggeration, you are
invited to spend decades getting constantly called a name that feels deeply
wrong with precisely zero recourse, and we can see how you feel at the end of
it. There's nothing fundamentally trans in this experience, but trans people
are the only folks who are valued so little in the current society that we are
required to endure it. What for most people is a mere thought exercise is our
lived reality.

Prelude out of the way, we can now move to the actual point of this article.

Many software systems are not exactly designed with the idea that some users
will change their names. This is probably mildly annoying at worst for most
people; maybe a cis woman who changes her maiden name has to change it and
gets to deal with some annoyance now and then. But, fundamentally, there is no
revocation of anyone's identity going on here; the person in question is still
mostly fine with seeing their old name here and there. Most of the annoyance
is just "oh, I have to change this."

For trans folks, though, it is a form of structural and institutional
transphobia. A poorly written software system will display your deadname,
front and center, and half the time changing it doesn't work and you have
to send someone a series of emails wherein you have to forcibly out yourself,
and more than likely, divulge your deadname. It's humiliating, and infuriating,
and such a massive pain that the system assumes you'll just give up, or better
yet, not bother the Important People with your problems in the first place and
instead simply grin and bear it. Changing your listed gender is also often the
same way.

Again, if you think I'm exaggerating, in the bit over half a year since I
legally changed my name, I've had to repeatedly email my university's tech
system because some new-fangled thing or form they dreamed up just, shows my
deadname for some reason. Or lists my legal gender as "male," when I'm
legally female. Or both. Usually it takes them a couple months worth of emails
before they finally get around to fixing it. And all this after I followed
their written, recommended procedure for name changes to the letter.

This is frankly unacceptable. Trans people have been considered a public topic
of discourse for at least a decade now, and it's not like we weren't there
before. I refuse to believe that I am the first trans person to ever have to
deal with these systems. I refuse to believe that it's that difficult to update
a single field; something that should be stored centrally anyways and should be
reasonably easy to update. I should not have to email you for two months to get
something that shouldn't even have been an issue in the first place fixed. I
don't even know where in their system they even dug up my deadname or
dead-gender; everything on the surface "looks" like it's changed successfully,
that information must just be buried in there somewhere, which is its own
transphobic kettle-of-fish. My uni isn't small either; it is a state school.
They have the resources to avoid these problems in the first place, just not
the will.

I don't care to opine on whether the folks designing and maintaining these
systems are, themselves, on a personal level, transphobic. The system itself
certainly is, though.

We don't even have to get in to the fact that, to even have a prayer of getting
my name changed, I had to go down to the local courthouse and pay hundreds
of dollars to legally change my name. I had to drive to another town (the
distance is too far to walk and public transit is nonexistent) to let the
Social Security Administration know I changed my name (they now restrict you
from changing your gender, but at least my state doesn't care what gender
SSA has you down as for anything, just the name). I had to get my drivers'
license updated. All of these things were only possible because I have a car,
a bit of extra money, and a bit of extra time to spend. These are things I'm
lucky to have, but they're not things everyone has, and a trans person who
doesn't have them is just out of luck, with zero ability to avoid getting
deadnamed.

So, if you're designing a computer system, or choosing a preexisting system
for your organization to use, or even vaguely associated with such a system,
it is your responsibility ask some questions. Does the system handle people
changing their names and genders? Does it handle people without middle and/or
last names? Does it let people set preferred names that will get used wherever
possible (ideally to the point of not needing a legal name at all)? If the
system provides things like emails or usernames that can sometimes contain a
person's name, can those be changed easily? Does the system let users know,
when they input a legal name, what that legal name will be used for? (This is
important, as sometimes someone can have legally changed
their name with certain government structures but have not gotten to others
yet. It's also good for privacy reasons.) Does the system let users avoid
showing any name anywhere (to the maximum extent possible)? (Some
pretransition trans folks don't want to see their deadname, but can't risk
others seeing their preferred name or haven't picked a preferred name yet.)
Can the system simultaneously handle all of the above? Test it, and make sure
it actually can.

I'm sick of not being considered a person by systems I interact with.
